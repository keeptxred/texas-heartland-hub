## Key takeaways

- Redistricting is the process of redrawing election-district boundaries. Reapportionment is different: it decides how many of the 435 U.S. House seats each state receives after the census.
- Texas normally redraws congressional and state legislative districts after the decennial census, but congressional districts can also be redrawn in the middle of a decade. Texas did that in 2003 and again in 2025.
- For the 2026 election, Texas is using congressional **PlanC2333**, enacted through House Bill 4 in 2025. The state House, state Senate and State Board of Education maps were not replaced in that mid-decade congressional redraw.
- The new congressional lines were designed to improve Republican opportunities in five Democratic-held districts. Republicans are favored in the redesigned 9th, 32nd and 35th districts and have stronger opportunities in the 28th and 34th, but map design does not guarantee an election result.
- The U.S. Supreme Court reversed the lower-court judgment that had blocked PlanC2333, so the map is controlling the 2026 congressional election. The broader Texas redistricting litigation is still important for elections after 2026.
- Redistricting law is also changing. The Supreme Court’s 2026 decision in *Louisiana v. Callais* tightened the framework for racial vote-dilution claims under Section 2 of the Voting Rights Act, which could affect future map challenges in Texas and elsewhere.

Redistricting is one of the most consequential parts of American elections that many voters rarely see. A district line can determine which communities vote together, which incumbents face one another, where campaigns spend money and whether a seat begins an election as competitive, safely Republican or safely Democratic.

Texas is a particularly important redistricting state because it is large, fast-growing, politically competitive in some metropolitan and border regions, and home to 38 U.S. House districts. It also has a long history of redistricting fights that move from the Legislature to federal court and, sometimes, all the way to the U.S. Supreme Court.

The 2026 election adds another chapter. Instead of waiting for the 2030 census, Texas lawmakers redrew the state’s congressional map in 2025. The resulting map, PlanC2333, is being used for the 2026 U.S. House elections after the Supreme Court reversed a lower-court ruling that had temporarily blocked it.

Understanding what happened requires separating four questions: how redistricting normally works, how Texas reached the current map, what the lines change for 2026, and what the legal and political precedent could mean for elections going forward.

## Redistricting and reapportionment are not the same thing

Every 10 years, the federal census counts the population. The 435 seats in the U.S. House are then **reapportioned** among the states according to population. After the 2020 census, Texas gained two seats, increasing its U.S. House delegation from 36 to 38 districts.

**Redistricting** comes next. It is the process of drawing the boundaries for those districts. Texas also redraws its 31 state Senate districts, 150 state House districts and 15 State Board of Education districts after the census.

Congressional districts must be drawn with nearly equal populations. Other federal protections also apply, including the Constitution and the Voting Rights Act. Texas law adds specific requirements for some state legislative maps, while congressional redistricting is principally an exercise of the Legislature’s general lawmaking power.

That distinction matters because reapportionment determines Texas’ number of seats; redistricting determines which Texans are grouped together inside each seat.

## Who draws the maps in Texas?

The Texas Legislature draws congressional districts as well as state House, state Senate and State Board of Education districts. The governor can sign or veto the resulting legislation.

There is an important difference if the Legislature fails to complete the ordinary post-census process. The Texas Constitution provides a Legislative Redistricting Board fallback for state House and state Senate districts. That board does not serve as the fallback for congressional or State Board of Education maps; a governor may instead call a special session, and courts can become involved when disputes or legal challenges arise.

The Legislature has substantial discretion about political and geographic choices so long as the final plan complies with governing federal and state law. Mapmakers can decide whether to keep communities together, split cities and counties, protect incumbents, create competitive districts or build districts that favor one political coalition over another. Some of those choices may be controversial without necessarily being unlawful.

## Why district lines can change election outcomes

Two redistricting techniques are commonly described as **packing** and **cracking**.

**Packing** concentrates a group of voters into a smaller number of districts. That group may win those districts by overwhelming margins but have less influence elsewhere.

**Cracking** divides a group among several districts so it lacks enough votes to control any one of them.

Those techniques can be used for partisan purposes, racial purposes or sometimes both, which is one reason redistricting lawsuits can become complicated. Courts may have to decide whether race predominated in drawing a map, whether minority voters have an equal electoral opportunity under federal law, and whether a state’s stated political objectives can be separated from racial data and effects.

For voters, the practical effect can be immediate. A person may keep the same home address but find that a new map places the neighborhood in a different congressional district with different candidates, different political geography and a different partisan history.

## Texas has fought over maps for generations

Redistricting litigation is not new in Texas. The modern history is a cycle of population growth, legislative mapmaking and court review.

### The Voting Rights Act era

Texas became fully covered by the federal Voting Rights Act’s Section 5 preclearance system in 1975. Under that system, covered jurisdictions generally had to obtain federal approval before implementing voting changes, including redistricting plans.

That requirement fundamentally shaped Texas mapmaking for decades. Federal officials and courts reviewed whether changes would unlawfully worsen minority voters’ electoral position or otherwise violate voting-rights protections.

### The 2003 mid-decade redraw

One of the clearest historical parallels to 2025 came in 2003.

After Republicans gained full control of the Texas Legislature, lawmakers pursued a new congressional map even though a post-2000-census map was already being used. The fight became nationally famous when Texas House Democrats left the state for Oklahoma in an attempt to deny the chamber a quorum. Senate Democrats later used a similar strategy.

The Legislature ultimately enacted a new congressional plan in a third called session in October 2003. The map helped Republicans build a stronger Texas congressional delegation, but the legal fight continued.

### *LULAC v. Perry* and the 2006 correction

In 2006, the U.S. Supreme Court ruled that Texas’ configuration of Congressional District 23 violated Section 2 of the Voting Rights Act. A federal court then ordered changes affecting five congressional districts for the 2006 election.

That case established an important lesson that still applies: partisan objectives do not erase federal voting-rights limits. A Legislature can pursue political goals, but a map can still fail if its treatment of voters violates federal law.

### *Shelby County v. Holder* changed the rules in 2013

The legal environment changed sharply in 2013. In *Shelby County v. Holder*, the U.S. Supreme Court held that the Voting Rights Act’s Section 4(b) coverage formula could no longer be used. The decision did not strike down Section 5 itself, but without the coverage formula, states including Texas were no longer automatically required to obtain federal preclearance before implementing new voting changes unless placed under a separate court order.

That shifted much of the practical burden from reviewing maps before they took effect to challenging them after enactment through litigation.

### The 2021 post-census maps

Texas added nearly four million residents from 2010 to 2020 and gained two congressional seats after the 2020 census, bringing the state to 38 U.S. House districts.

The Republican-controlled Legislature enacted new congressional, state House, state Senate and State Board of Education maps in 2021. Those maps generated federal lawsuits alleging racial discrimination and vote dilution. Texas disputed those claims and defended the plans.

The 2021 congressional map, PlanC2193, governed the 2022 and 2024 election cycles.

## Why Texas redrew Congress again in 2025

Texas did not wait for the 2030 census to revisit its congressional districts.

Gov. Greg Abbott called a special session in July 2025 that included congressional redistricting. The first called session ended without final passage. A second called session began immediately afterward, and lawmakers passed House Bill 4 on August 23, 2025. Abbott signed it on August 29.

House Bill 4 enacted **PlanC2333** for congressional elections beginning with the 2026 primary and general election.

The political objective was unusually explicit: the new map was crafted to improve Republican chances of gaining as many as five U.S. House seats. Democratic lawmakers and voting-rights groups challenged the plan, arguing in part that race had unlawfully driven important district choices. Texas argued that the map reflected lawful partisan objectives rather than unconstitutional racial line-drawing.

In November 2025, a divided three-judge federal panel preliminarily blocked the map. The U.S. Supreme Court stayed that ruling in December, allowing Texas to proceed with PlanC2333 for the approaching campaign. On April 27, 2026, the Supreme Court went further and summarily reversed the lower court’s judgment.

For the 2026 election, that question is settled operationally: **PlanC2333 is the congressional map in effect.**

## What changed for the 2026 election?

The 2025 legislation changed **congressional districts**, not the state House, state Senate or State Board of Education maps.

Texas’ official redistricting site lists PlanC2333 as the U.S. House plan for the 2026 elections. The state Senate remains PlanS2168, the state House remains PlanH2316 and the State Board of Education remains PlanE2106.

There is also a transition that can confuse voters: members of Congress elected under the older PlanC2193 boundaries continue representing those districts through the end of the current Congress. The representatives elected in November 2026 under PlanC2333 take office when the next Congress begins in January 2027.

In other words, a voter’s **2026 ballot district** can be based on the new map while the voter’s **current representative** is still serving a district drawn under the old map.

## The five districts at the center of the 2026 strategy

The new map was designed to improve Republican performance in five Democratic-held districts: the 9th, 28th, 32nd, 34th and 35th.

The political effects are not identical in each seat.

### Districts 9, 32 and 35

The redesigned 9th, 32nd and 35th districts moved from strongly Democratic configurations toward electorates that favored Donald Trump in the 2024 presidential vote. That turned seats represented by Democrats into Republican-favored opportunities under the new lines.

The 32nd District is the most dramatic example. Its new geography stretches from the Dallas suburbs into more Republican territory in East Texas. Analysis of the new boundaries found that a district Kamala Harris carried by roughly 24 points in 2024 would instead have favored Trump by roughly 18 points.

That is not a small adjustment around the edges. It changes the basic political character of the district and therefore changes which candidates are likely to enter, where national parties spend money and what kind of coalition a winning campaign needs.

### Districts 28 and 34

The 28th and 34th districts in South Texas were already competitive enough to attract Republican attention. The 2025 redraw strengthened the Republican position further.

Under the new lines, Trump would have carried both districts by about 10 percentage points based on the 2024 presidential vote. Democratic incumbents Henry Cuellar in the 28th and Vicente Gonzalez in the 34th therefore face electorates designed to be more difficult for Democrats than the prior map.

Again, historical presidential results are a benchmark, not a prediction. Congressional candidates can run ahead of or behind the top of the ticket, and turnout patterns in a midterm can differ sharply from a presidential election.

## How redistricting changes the 2026 campaign

The effect of the map goes beyond five sets of boundary lines.

First, it changes **candidate decisions**. Incumbents may retire, move into another race or face a colleague in a primary. New candidates may enter when a district suddenly becomes favorable to their party.

Second, it changes **campaign money**. National parties, political committees and donors allocate resources partly according to which seats appear winnable. A district transformed from safe Democratic to Republican-leaning can attract an entirely different field and spending profile.

Third, it changes **turnout strategy**. Campaigns build field operations around the voters actually inside their district. Moving suburban precincts, rural counties or parts of a city across a boundary changes the electorate a campaign must contact and persuade.

Fourth, it affects the **national fight for the U.S. House**. A map capable of producing several additional Republican seats in Texas can matter when the congressional majority is narrow. That was a major reason the 2025 redraw became a national political story rather than a Texas-only dispute.

But the fifth effect is a warning against treating a map as an election result. Redistricting can improve the odds for a party, but it cannot control candidate quality, scandals, national political conditions, turnout, fundraising or shifts in voter preference. Republicans designed PlanC2333 to create opportunities. Voters still decide whether those opportunities become victories.

## The map also creates risk for the party drawing it

Aggressive redistricting involves tradeoffs.

To create additional favorable districts, mapmakers often have to move reliable voters out of districts where their party previously had large margins. That can make some incumbent districts less overwhelmingly safe. A strategy that works in a normal political environment can become less comfortable in a strong wave election.

That is why the most useful way to evaluate PlanC2333 is not simply to count how many districts Trump would have won in 2024. Analysts should also ask how each district performed in other statewide elections, how its electorate changes in midterms, whether congressional candidates historically run differently from presidential candidates, and whether demographic or partisan coalitions are still moving in the same direction.

South Texas is a good example. Republican gains among Hispanic voters have changed the region’s politics, but individual Democratic congressional candidates have at times outperformed the Democratic presidential ticket. The new map is a bet that recent Republican gains are durable enough to translate into House victories.

## What does the Supreme Court ruling mean?

The April 27, 2026 Supreme Court action removed the lower-court judgment that had blocked the new map. As a result, Texas is using PlanC2333 throughout the 2026 election cycle.

That does **not** mean every legal question involving Texas redistricting has disappeared. The consolidated litigation involving Texas’ 2021 and 2025 maps has included multiple constitutional and Voting Rights Act claims, and legal trackers continue to list proceedings over the merits of those maps.

The distinction is important. The operative map for November 2026 is no longer realistically in doubt, but what courts ultimately say about the map’s legality and what remedies may apply to later elections can still matter.

## A second 2026 Supreme Court ruling may matter even more for future maps

Two days after the Texas redistricting decision, the Supreme Court issued *Louisiana v. Callais*.

The Court did not eliminate Section 2 of the Voting Rights Act, but it tightened the standards governing racial vote-dilution claims in redistricting. Among other things, the decision requires plaintiffs to do more to separate race from ordinary partisan voting behavior and places greater emphasis on present-day evidence supporting a strong inference of racial discrimination.

That matters in Texas because race and party preference often overlap statistically in ways that make map litigation difficult. Going forward, plaintiffs challenging a map will face a more demanding task when arguing that minority voters have been unlawfully denied an equal opportunity to elect their preferred candidates.

At the same time, the Constitution still restricts states from making race the predominant factor in drawing districts without adequate legal justification. Mapmakers therefore face pressure from both directions: they cannot ignore applicable voting-rights protections, but they also cannot use race too aggressively as the organizing principle of a map.

That tension is likely to shape the next generation of Texas redistricting litigation.

## Could Texas redraw the map again before 2030?

Yes, it is possible.

Texas’ 2003 and 2025 experiences demonstrate that congressional redistricting is not necessarily limited to the once-a-decade period immediately after the census. No current Texas rule automatically locks a congressional map in place until the next census simply because it has already been used in an election.

Whether lawmakers choose to redraw again is a political and legal question. A future Legislature and governor could revisit congressional boundaries, while a court could also order changes if a map is ultimately found unlawful.

That means voters should treat “the map for the decade” as a common expectation, not an absolute guarantee.

## What happens after the 2030 census?

The next regular redistricting cycle begins after the 2030 census.

Congressional seats will again be reapportioned among the states. Texas’ representation could grow, shrink or stay at 38 depending on how its population changes relative to the rest of the country. Once detailed census data becomes available, Texas will draw a new set of congressional and state districts for the following elections.

Population growth will again matter. Fast-growing suburbs, expanding metropolitan corridors, the Rio Grande Valley and changing rural populations can alter where districts must gain or lose residents to remain equally populated.

The 2030 process will therefore not begin with a blank political slate. It will inherit the legal precedents, demographic changes, partisan strategies and mid-decade precedent established during the 2020s.

## The national effect: mid-decade redistricting may no longer be exceptional

Texas’ 2025 redraw also helped accelerate a national redistricting fight. Other states considered or enacted their own mid-decade changes in response, turning district maps into another front in the competition for control of Congress.

That creates a broader strategic problem for both parties. If one state redraws aggressively to gain seats, leaders in another state may feel pressure to respond. The result can be a cycle in which congressional maps become less stable between censuses.

For voters, stability has value. Districts that remain recognizable allow communities to build relationships with representatives and give voters time to understand who represents them. For political parties, however, control of a closely divided House creates a powerful incentive to maximize every legal mapmaking advantage.

The 2025 Texas precedent ensures that those competing interests will remain part of the redistricting debate.

## The Keep TX Red bottom line

Texas redistricting should be evaluated on more than whether Republicans or Democrats gain seats.

Three separate questions matter.

**Is the map legal?** Courts must apply the Constitution and the Voting Rights Act as the Supreme Court defines them.

**Is the map politically effective?** PlanC2333 plainly improves Republican opportunities in several districts, but the 2026 voters will determine whether the strategy works.

**Does the process produce durable representation?** Texans benefit from knowing which communities share a district, who is accountable for drawing the lines and why boundaries changed.

For 2026, the practical answer is straightforward: PlanC2333 is the congressional map, and it gives Republicans a stronger opportunity to expand their U.S. House delegation.

For elections after 2026, the answer is less settled. Ongoing litigation, a newly tightened Voting Rights Act framework, the possibility of additional mid-decade action and the approaching 2030 census all mean Texas’ district lines will remain a major political and legal issue.

The most important point is also the simplest: redistricting sets the field, but it does not cast the ballots. Maps can shape political opportunity. Voters still decide the election.

## Frequently asked questions

### What congressional map is Texas using in the 2026 election?

Texas is using PlanC2333, the congressional map enacted through House Bill 4 in 2025. The U.S. Supreme Court reversed the lower-court judgment that had blocked the plan, leaving it in effect for the 2026 congressional elections.

### Did Texas redraw state House and state Senate districts for 2026 too?

No. The 2025 mid-decade legislation redrew the U.S. House map. Texas’ official redistricting site lists the state Senate, state House and State Board of Education plans used since the 2022 elections as continuing in effect for 2026.

### Is redistricting the same as gerrymandering?

No. Redistricting is the required or legally authorized act of drawing district boundaries. Gerrymandering describes manipulating those boundaries to achieve a political or other advantage. Every gerrymander is a form of redistricting, but not every redistricting plan is necessarily a gerrymander.

### Can Texas legally redistrict in the middle of a decade?

Texas has done so before, most notably for congressional districts in 2003 and again in 2025. Congressional redistricting is not automatically limited to one enactment after each census, although any new map remains subject to applicable constitutional and federal statutory limits.

### Does the 2026 map guarantee five more Republican members of Congress?

No. The map was designed to create or strengthen five Republican pickup opportunities, but district partisanship is not a guarantee. Candidate quality, turnout, fundraising, national conditions and changing voter preferences can all alter the outcome.

### When will Texas redistrict again?

The next regular cycle follows the 2030 census, but congressional lines could change earlier through new legislation or a court order. The pending legal questions surrounding Texas maps also mean future elections should be monitored rather than assuming the 2026 boundaries are permanent through 2030.
