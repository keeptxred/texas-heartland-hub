# Texas DTPA notice and limitations — source-backed no-change check

Checked October 28, 2026 for the existing [consumer protection guide](https://keeptxred.com/laws/topic/consumer-protection-deceptive-trade-law): `src/data/law-topic-consumer-protection-authority.ts`.

**Narrow question:** Are the currently displayed explanations of the 60-day presuit written notice rule, notice exceptions/abatement and the DTPA limitations period materially correct?

## Primary source

[Current Texas Business & Commerce Code Chapter 17, §§17.505(a)–(e) and 17.565](https://tcss.legis.texas.gov/resources/BC/htm/BC.17.htm), checked against the published statutory text.

- **§17.505(a):** A consumer pursuing the covered §17.50(b)(1) damages claim ordinarily must give the defendant written notice **at least 60 days before filing**, reasonably detailing the specific complaint and amounts of economic loss, mental anguish damage and reasonably incurred expenses/attorney fees. This is **not** a universal notice duty for every consumer complaint or every possible legal cause of action.
- **§17.505(b):** Notice is excused when giving it is impracticable because suit must be filed to avoid expiration of limitations, or when the DTPA claim is asserted as a counterclaim. The separate tender procedure remains.
- **§17.505(c)–(e):** An eligible defendant may request abatement, normally by a timely plea within 30 days after answering; a qualifying uncontroverted verified plea can cause automatic abatement on the 11th day; the statutory abatement generally continues until 60 days after proper notice.
- **§17.565:** A DTPA action must generally be commenced within two years after the deceptive act or when the consumer discovered or reasonably should have discovered it. A **conditional 180-day extension**, not an automatic extension, requires proof that a defendant knowingly engaged in conduct solely calculated to induce delayed filing.

## Comparison and result

The guide already states the 60-day notice, the limits-preserving and counterclaim exceptions, abatement possibility, two-year discovery-based clock and narrowly conditional 180-day extension in its framework, key rules and two directly relevant FAQs. It does **not** suggest that a generic customer-service request automatically meets statutory notice, that all private claims always require this notice, or that a notice letter restarts limitations.
